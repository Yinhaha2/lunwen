\section{Introduction} \label{sec:intro}
\label{intro}

The integration of LLMs into software development tools has introduced a new era of AI-powered coding assistants. These LLM-based tools, such as GitHub Copilot, Copilot Chat, ChatGPT, and CodeWhisperer, have moved past mere inline completion. Autonomous coding agents (e.g., Claude Code, Cursor, Devin, and OpenAI Codex) now open pull requests (PRs), respond to reviews, and retry continuous integration (CI) directly on real-world repositories.

While tools like Copilot and DeepSeek-Coder offer the potential to enhance developer productivity, ensuring the quality of the generated code remains a crucial area of investigation. Prior research has extensively studied and reported challenges related to correctness~\citep{DBLP:conf/msr/NguyenN22,wang2025understandingcharacteristicscodegeneration}, security and privacy associated with code generated by LLMs. However, performance engineering introduces a fundamentally different challenge. A performance PR asks maintainers to believe a claim about runtime, build cost, or resource use, which requires specific evidence and validation beyond static correctness.

Autonomous coding agents now submit agentic PRs at scale and are demonstrably weaker than humans on acceptance, especially on performance tasks. Maintainers already treat these agent-authored PRs as part of their ordinary review load, yet the underlying mechanisms of why these PRs succeed or fail remain opaque.

Prior work on agentic performance PRs reports a rejection-rate penalty (36.5\% vs. 22.7\%) and builds a topic taxonomy. However, treating PR outcomes as a simple binary (merged vs. closed) is misleading. A closed PR may be a documented technical veto, an expired stale bot, or an author who abandoned the thread. A merged PR may be a rigorously reviewed optimization or a five-minute quick merge of a small patch that nobody measured. If merge rate alone is treated as the quality metric, we fail to understand the real friction points.

To fill this gap, we study 1,219 agent-authored performance PRs across 446 GitHub repositories and decompose both ends of the outcome distribution. We combine GitHub artifacts with a structured LLM analysis that labels optimization layers, review concerns, and capability boundaries. Specifically, our study aims to answer four research questions (RQs):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1:} What is the outcome landscape of agent-authored performance PRs?
    \item \textbf{RQ2:} How do agent-authored performance PRs get merged under different levels of review attention?
    \item \textbf{RQ3:} What are the root causes and capability boundaries of failed agent-authored performance PRs?
    \item \textbf{RQ4:} How can the outcomes of these performance PRs be improved?
\end{itemize}

To facilitate research reproducibility, we make the original datasets and scripts available in our replication package\footnote{link}. 

Our contributions are summarized below:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Performance assessment of agent-generated PRs:} We systematically evaluated the outcome landscape of 1,219 agentic performance PRs, reporting a terminal merge rate of 56.9\%. We demonstrate that both outcomes are internally heterogeneous: 75.6\% of merges follow a low-friction/fast path without formal review, while 55.4\% of closures are caused by silent abandonment rather than technical rejections.
    \item \textbf{Root causes of AI-generated code failures:} We qualitatively analyzed the root causes of performance PR rejections. We found that observable validation is dominated by static code reading. For PRs that receive formal reviews, failures concentrate on functional correctness and design. Furthermore, evidence generation (e.g., missing benchmarks) and process workflows form the primary capability boundaries, dropping merge rates to as low as 12.5\%.
    \item \textbf{Actionable implications for performance PRs:} We explored the differences between merged and closed PRs to provide path-specific strategies. We highlight that routine technical-stack edits merge easily without heavy measurement, whereas complex PRs fail without reproducible evidence. This finding refutes the naive assumption that adding a benchmark to every PR improves acceptance, suggesting instead targeted toolchain enhancements based on the PR's capability boundary.
\end{itemize}
