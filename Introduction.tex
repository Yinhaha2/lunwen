\section{Introduction} \label{sec:intro}
\label{intro}
The integration of LLMs into software development tools has introduced a new era of AI-powered coding assistants. These LLM-based tools, such as GitHub Copilot, Copilot Chat, ChatGPT, and CodeWhisperer, as well as code generation models such as CodeLlama and DeepSeek-Coder, generate code from natural-language prompts and have been rapidly adopted in daily development.
While tools like Copilot and DeepSeek-Coder offer the potential to enhance developer productivity, ensuring the quality of the generated code remains a crucial area of investigation. Prior research has extensively studied and reported challenges related to correctness~\citep{DBLP:conf/msr/NguyenN22,wang2025understandingcharacteristicscodegeneration}, security~\citep{pearce2022asleep}, and privacy associated with code generated by LLMs. More recent work has examined function-level efficiency on programming benchmarks~\citep{li2026performance,liu2024evalperf}. Those studies answer whether a snippet is right, safe, or faster on a controlled input.

Autonomous coding agents now submit agentic pull requests (PRs) at scale~\citep{li2026aidev}. Tools such as GitHub Copilot, Claude Code, Cursor, Devin, and OpenAI Codex differ in interface, but they share a new unit of work---the agent-authored PR---whose recorded outcome is merge or close. Maintainers already treat those PRs as part of ordinary review load, and merge rate has become the headline metric for whether the agents succeed. Prior work on agentic performance PRs reports a rejection-rate penalty (36.5\% vs.\ 22.7\%) and builds a topic taxonomy of the optimizations agents attempt~\citep{opu2025agentic}.

However, a rate and a taxonomy still leave the mechanism opaque. A performance PR asks maintainers to believe a claim about runtime, build cost, or resource use, which is a different technical and social act from accepting a small refactor. A closed PR may be a documented technical veto, an expired stale bot, or an author who abandoned the thread. A merged PR may be a reviewed optimization, or a five-minute merge of a small patch that nobody measured. If merge is treated as quality, agents will be rewarded for patches that never entered a performance-engineering check, and blamed for closures that never received a human sentence.

To fill this gap, we study 1,219 agent-authored performance PRs written by five agents in 446 GitHub repositories, and decompose both ends of the outcome distribution. We combine GitHub artifacts with a structured LLM analysis that labels optimization layer, review concern, reproducibility of materials, and an analytic capability boundary. In particular, we answer four research questions on the outcome landscape of these PRs, how they get merged, the root causes of failed PRs, and how their outcomes can be improved. We find a terminal merge rate of 56.9\% (671/1,180), but the average is internally heterogeneous: 75.6\% of merges follow a low-friction path, and 55.4\% of closures are silent abandonment rather than written rejection. Successful PRs are short-lived and lightly reviewed; when validation is observable, it is dominated by code reading rather than profiling. After restricting attention to closures with review or a rejection signal (195/509), the leading failures are functional correctness and design. Process and evidence boundaries stratify terminal merge rates from 79.6\% to 12.5\%. Merged and closed PRs separate first by lifetime and boundary type, not by who attached a benchmark table.

To facilitate research reproducibility, we make the filtered corpus, scripts, and annotation schema available in our replication package.\footnote{link}
Our contributions are summarized below.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Performance assessment of agent-generated PRs:} We systematically evaluate 1,219 agent-authored performance PRs and report a terminal merge rate of 56.9\% (671/1,180), with large descriptive gaps across agents. We show that both merge and close are internally heterogeneous: 75.6\% of merges follow a low-friction path, and 55.4\% of closures are silent abandonment.
    \item \textbf{Root causes of failed agent-generated PRs:} We qualitatively analyze the reviewed-or-rejected subset (195/509) and find that failures concentrate on functional correctness and design, rather than on inefficiency antipatterns. Process and evidence boundaries stratify terminal merge rates from 79.6\% to 12.5\%.
    \item \textbf{Path-specific implications for agents and maintainers:} We separate a fast path, on which routine technical-stack edits already merge without measurement, from a slow path, on which missing reproducible evidence and weak in-PR repair keep PRs closed. Adding a benchmark to every performance PR is not supported by the material signals.
\end{itemize}
